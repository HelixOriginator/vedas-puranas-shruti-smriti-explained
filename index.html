Three questions people actually ask about Sanatana Dharma — answered by tracing where timeless law ends and lived story begins.

A field note from public queries, worked through with Vedic and Puranic reasoning.

Why a tradition needs both the law and the story

If the Vedas already hold the truth, what are the Puranas actually for?

The Vedas and the Puranas aren't competing accounts of the same thing — they're two different jobs. One states the law. The other shows the law living inside a human life.

MeaningFrom vid, "to know" — foundational, ultimate truth."Ancient," or that which renews and completes.
ContentPhilosophy, eternal law, mantra, universal truth.Narrative, genealogy, allegory, ritual.

Theory needs a place to land

The Vedas describe cosmic order — Rta — and the nature of ultimate reality. That's the raw material. The Puranas take that material and place it inside a person's dilemma: what does righteousness look like when Rama has to choose between duty and love, or when Krishna has to counsel a warrior who doesn't want to fight?

The recipe and the meal. A recipe is correct and complete on the page, but nobody eats a recipe. The Purana is the meal made from the Vedic recipe — the same nourishment, now something you can actually taste.

Two minds, two doors

An intellectual seeker asking "what is the nature of reality" is met by Upanishadic dialogue. A seeker asking "how do I feel close to the divine" is met by a temple, a festival, a story with a face. Sanatana Dharma keeps both doors open rather than making everyone enter through one.

Vedas without Puranas

Philosophy risks turning cold — academically correct, but distant from daily devotion and ethics.

Puranas without Vedas

Story and ritual risk hardening into literalism, cut loose from the principle they were built to express.

The Vedas provide the foundation. The Puranas provide the living expression on top of it.

Shruti & Smriti what is heard / what is remembered

If the Vedas know everything, why no dinosaurs?

A question that sounds like a contradiction, until the categories are separated.

The confusion disappears once you stop expecting the Vedas to be a list of things and start reading them as a description of laws.

A law is invisible, a symbol is local

Shruti — the Vedas and Upanishads — carries the principle: universal claims about consciousness, ultimate reality, and cosmic order. Smriti — the Puranas — carries the expression: how that principle gets dressed in the social rules, rituals, and imagery of a particular time and place.

Not a zoological catalog

The Vedas aren't a field guide to every creature that ever lived. When they mention a cow, a horse, a bird, it's to teach something or to symbolize something — never to classify it. Their questions run deeper than "how many legs does this have": where did the universe come from, what animates a living thing, what keeps the seasons in order.

The physics comparison. When a physicist says physics explains the whole universe, they mean the underlying laws and particles — not that a textbook lists every dinosaur and every kangaroo by name. The Vedas work the same way: they hold the principle behind creation, not an inventory of everything created.

Iconography is geography

A deity's mount — lion, peacock, bull, serpent — is a Puranic, Smriti-level choice, drawn from the flora and fauna the tradition actually lived among. Had the same principles first taken root in Australia, the imagery might ride a kangaroo. In the Arctic, a polar bear. Dinosaurs went extinct long before any human civilization built a regional symbolism at all — so their absence isn't a gap in the Vedas' knowledge, it's simply outside the job iconography was ever doing.

The Vedas hold the principle behind everything that exists. The stories hold pictures of what the tradition could see. No contradiction — just two different kinds of truth, asked to do two different jobs.

Asanga the unattached witness

If God is in everyone, what about evil?

The question children ask first, and adults never quite stop asking.

If the divine is present everywhere, is it also present in a cruel act, a filthy place, a person doing real harm? And if so, why does nothing stop it?

Why "everywhere" follows logically

Trace anything back far enough and it depends on something prior — bread on flour, flour on wheat, wheat on rain and sun. That chain has to terminate somewhere independent; Vedic philosophy calls that terminus Brahman. And because an effect carries the qualities of its cause, whatever Brahman causes must, in some sense, contain Brahman. That's the argument for presence everywhere, including the places nobody wants it to be.

Light through dirty water. Shine a light through a glass of muddy water — the light passes straight through and reaches the other side untouched by the mud. God is described the same way: asanga, unattached. Present inside every condition, but never stained by it.

Power given, not choices made

The sun lights a food crop and a poison plant without picking a side. Electricity runs a reading lamp and a neon sign the same way. The divine is described as supplying life-energy on the same impartial terms — not authoring each choice made with it.

Karma does the accounting

Rather than intervening case by case, the system is set up so actions generate their own consequences over time — across more than one lifetime, in a view long enough that human impatience isn't the right lens to judge it by.

Presence isn't permission, and stillness isn't indifference. The light stays clean; the water still has to clear on its own schedule.
